\title{StableMax-PPO: Best-of-\(N\) RLHF with\protect\\Ordinal Reward Distributions}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Best-of-\(N\) systems generate \(N\) responses at deployment, whereas
reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) usually optimizes the expected
reward of one response. Scalar finite-budget objectives partly close this gap,
but discard each response's probabilities of receiving different ratings.
Equal mean ratings can therefore conceal different expected maxima within a
candidate group. We introduce \textbf{\method{}}, which retains these probabilities
from a frozen ordinal reward model trained on human ratings and optimizes the
expected best latent rating among \(N\) candidates. A fixed, one-sided bound on
the cumulative rating distribution gives an exact worst-case expected maximum
and an exact response-level credit for proximal policy optimization (PPO).
Predictive variance remains a model-based diagnostic of annotator disagreement,
not a reward bonus. Lower bounds on mean rating and an independently trained
quality score constrain typical responses; Kullback--Leibler (KL) regularization
and rollback limit policy drift. Across three seeds at \(N=32\),
\textbf{\method{}} attains a robust maximum of 3.458 with held-out KL of 0.034,
compared with 3.424 and 0.070 for Mean-PPO. Group relative policy optimization (GRPO) and scalar max-PPO achieve
higher robust maxima at higher KL. The results establish a lower-drift
operating point under frozen evaluators, not uniform improvement across
endpoints or validation with fresh human judgments.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Many language-model systems generate \(N\) responses and then return, rerank,
or inspect the best candidate \citep{nakano2021webgpt,cobbe2021training}.
Standard RLHF instead optimizes the expected score of one response
\citep{christiano2017deep,stiennon2020learning,ouyang2022training}.
A higher mean score need not make a highly rated candidate more likely within
this fixed budget. Mean-PPO and group relative policy optimization (GRPO) are
general-purpose methods \citep{shao2024deepseekmath,guo2025deepseekr1}; recent
$\mathrm{max}@k$, Best-of-\(N\), and inference-aware policy gradients target
multi-response performance \citep{chow2025inference,tang2025inference,
walder2025pkpo,bagirov2025best,li2026posttraining,poiani2026maxk}.
Their scalar-reward formulations nevertheless reduce each response to one
score. Equal means can conceal different probabilities of receiving high
ratings, and thus different expected rating maxima.
Upper-tail optimization can also amplify calibration error, degrade typical
responses, or increase policy drift
\citep{gao2023scaling,kirk2024understanding}.

We introduce \method{}, which converts rating probabilities into a group
objective and a training signal for each response. A frozen ordinal reward
model (Moment RM), trained from repeated HelpSteer2 helpfulness ratings
\citep{wang2024helpsteer2}, predicts probabilities over \(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\).
Its mean measures typical model-scored quality; its variance is a model-based
diagnostic of annotator disagreement, not a reward bonus. The cumulative distribution
function (CDF) gives the probability of a rating at or below each threshold.
Raising these CDFs within a fixed bound makes high ratings less likely and
exactly attains the worst-case expected maximum over the declared product
ambiguity set. The same probabilities give each response's exact marginal
credit: how much it adds to the group's expected maximum.

The technical distinction is that CDF robustification changes both the group
objective and its policy credit. PPO implements the update. A nominal-mean
floor and a separately trained, frozen quality reward model (Quality RM) from
preference pairs constrain typical model-scored quality. Reference-policy KL
regularization and state-complete rollback control accepted updates. Maximum,
both quality measurements, and drift remain separate reported quantities.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Using rating distributions for finite-budget alignment.}
We retain all five rating probabilities from repeated human annotations to
compute the expected maximum. The mean measures typical model-scored quality;
the variance remains a disagreement diagnostic. The contribution is this
interface for policy optimization, not a new ordinal regression model.

\item \textbf{An exact robust objective and policy credit.}
We derive the worst-case expected maximum over a one-sided product CDF
ambiguity set and its leave-one-out credit
(\Cref{thm:main-max,thm:main-gradient}). The score-function estimator is
unbiased before PPO clipping. A true-maximum lower bound holds under conditional
coverage (\Cref{thm:coverage-summary}). Value and credit cost
\(O(N|\mathcal R|)\) without sampling latent ratings.

\item \textbf{A constrained PPO realization with explicit safeguards.}
Adaptive dual weights target nominal-mean and independently trained quality
floors. KL control regulates policy drift; state-complete rollback restores
rejected policy and optimizer updates, leaving controller state unchanged. These
safeguards do not imply a population KL guarantee.

\item \textbf{A three-seed characterization at a fixed response budget.}
At \(N=32\), \method{} attains robust maximum 3.458 and held-out KL 0.034,
versus 3.424 and 0.070 for Mean-PPO. GRPO and scalar max-PPO attain higher maxima
at higher drift. Matched comparators, component ablations, and independent
model-based diagnostics characterize this operating point, not a complete
Pareto frontier or superiority on fresh human judgments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Post-training and inference-time selection.}
Learning rewards from preferences underlies RLHF
\citep{christiano2017deep,stiennon2020learning,ouyang2022training}; DPO
reparameterizes a KL-regularized preference objective without online policy
optimization \citep{rafailov2023direct}. GRPO uses within-group reward
normalization for post-training \citep{shao2024deepseekmath,guo2025deepseekr1}.
At inference time, verifier-guided selection, answer aggregation, and adaptive
compute allocation exploit multiple candidate responses
\citep{cobbe2021training,wang2023selfconsistency,snell2025scaling}.
These procedures differ in what constitutes a successful group. Our setting
fixes the response budget and targets its expected ordinal rating maximum;
it does not optimize majority voting or allocate compute across prompts.

\textbf{Finite-budget training objectives.}
Best-of-\(N\)-aware fine-tuning \citep{chow2025inference}, generic
inference-time objectives \citep{tang2025inference}, pass@\(k\) policy
gradients \citep{walder2025pkpo}, and max@\(k\) optimization
\citep{bagirov2025best} align training with multi-candidate evaluation.
InfAlign derives reward transformations for inference procedures
\citep{balashankar2024infalign}, while Compute Aligned Training derives
procedure-dependent losses \citep{ousherovitch2026compute}.
\citet{li2026posttraining} address a different limitation: they extrapolate
scalar reward tails when training uses fewer rollouts than deployment.
\citet{poiani2026maxk} study policy structure and sample complexity for
max@\(k\) in finite-horizon RL. Our contribution concerns the distribution
\emph{within each response}: conditional ordinal rating probabilities define
an exact robust group value and its credit at a matched budget. It neither
replaces these broader inference-aware frameworks nor claims to solve the
training--deployment budget mismatch.

\textbf{Ordinal, probabilistic, and distributional rewards.}
Ordinal regression provides ordered-category models
\citep{mccullagh1980regression}, while HelpSteer2 supplies graded human
annotations for reward modeling \citep{wang2024helpsteer2}. Uncertainty-aware
and probabilistic reward models represent information beyond a point score
\citep{lou2024uncertainty,sun2025purm}; predictive variation and epistemic
uncertainty are distinct concepts \citep{kendall2017uncertainties}.
Proper scoring rules and calibration assess the predictive probabilities
\citep{gneiting2007strictly,guo2017calibration}. Distributional RL instead
models return distributions over trajectories
\citep{bellemare2017distributional,dabney2018distributional}.
Here the frozen distribution is over annotations conditional on a completed
response. Its CDF enters a finite-budget policy objective, while its variance
is neither an epistemic confidence measure nor an exploration bonus.

\textbf{Robustness and reward-model overoptimization.}
Robust MDP formulations optimize against uncertainty sets over transition
models \citep{iyengar2005robust,nilim2005robust}. We use a product ambiguity
set over conditional rating CDFs; its exact solution relies on stochastic
ordering and conditional independence rather than dynamic programming.
Reward-model overoptimization motivates safeguards against optimizing a proxy
beyond its reliable range \citep{gao2023scaling}, and reward-model ensembles
can mitigate this problem \citep{coste2024reward}. Our one-sided CDF envelope
modifies the objective and credit together, but its true-rating lower bound
requires conditional coverage. Neither calibration on held-out prompt
clusters nor agreement between model-based evaluators establishes that
coverage for every generated response.

\textbf{Diversity and constrained policy optimization.}
RLHF can reduce output diversity \citep{kirk2024understanding}; deliberate
representation-based exploration can improve multi-sample coverage
\citep{tuyls2026representation}. Our credit rewards incremental rating-threshold
coverage, without adding a semantic-diversity or variance bonus.
PPO and trust-region methods provide policy-update mechanisms
\citep{schulman2017proximal,schulman2015trust}, and constrained policy
optimization separates reward objectives from constraints
\citep{achiam2017constrained}. We use adaptive quality-floor penalties and
empirical KL control with rollback. This implementation does not inherit a
population feasibility or monotonic-improvement guarantee from those methods.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:problem-setup}

\subsection{Setting and assumptions}
We study response-level RLHF with a fixed Best-of-\(N\) generation budget.  The
policy parameter \(\theta\) is trainable, whereas the ordinal Moment RM \(\omega\),
scalar Quality RM \(\eta\), and reference policy \(\pi_{\rm ref}\) are frozen.
For \(x\sim\mathcal D\), responses factor as
\(\pi_\theta(Y\mid x)=\prod_h\pi_\theta(a_h\mid x,a_{<h})\), and a rollout
contains \(Y_{1:N}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\).  We
assume (A1) conditional independence of rating outcomes across candidates, (A2)
frozen evaluators and radius, and (A3) differentiable policies with common
support and integrable scores permitting differentiation under expectation. Each response
receives one terminal evaluation; the group statistic is formed before any
response-level credit is assigned.

Assumption (A1) concerns annotation outcomes conditional on completed
responses; it does not assert token independence.  It is what converts each
threshold event for the maximum into a product of candidate CDFs.  Assumption
(A2) keeps both evaluators and the reference policy fixed, so the measured
operating point changes only through the policy and controller.  Assumption (A3) enables
the likelihood-ratio differentiation in Theorem~\ref{thm:main-gradient}.
Correlated evaluator errors are therefore outside the product model rather than
an effect absorbed by the optimizer.

\subsection{Ordinal evaluator and finite-budget value}
The Moment RM returns \(p_{\omega,r}(x,y)=\Pr_\omega(R=r\mid x,y)\) for
\(\mathcal R=\{0,1,2,3,4\}\); write \(\Delta(\mathcal R)\) for the probability
simplex on these five levels.  Its mean
\(\mu_\omega=\sum_r rp_{\omega,r}\) measures target-RM quality, while
\(\sigma_\omega^2=\sum_r(r-\mu_\omega)^2p_{\omega,r}\) is retained only as a
model-based disagreement diagnostic; the independent scalar \(Q_\eta(x,y)\) is a
separate quality check.  The category endpoints are fixed by the training data;
the ordinal-probit parameterization is given in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}.
The five levels are fixed by the evaluator interface, not selected after
observing policy outcomes.

For candidate \(j\), let \(F_{\omega,j}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{t}p_{\omega,r}(x,Y_j)\).
Under (A1), the nominal finite-support identity is
\(h_N^{\rm nom}=\sum_{t=1}^{4}[1-\prod_{j=1}^{N}F_{\omega,j}(t-1)]\), and
\(J_N^{\rm nom}(\theta)=\mathbb E[h_N^{\rm nom}]\).  Its threshold products
show why the full ordinal vector matters: a candidate is useful when the other
responses leave a threshold uncovered, which a scalar maximum cannot represent
from response means alone. The identity integrates out rating noise exactly;
the top-rating event is only one of the four threshold terms.

This objective measures the expected best latent rating in a candidate group.
It is not the expected rating of a response selected by its predicted mean:
the maximum is taken over rating outcomes before integrating them out.
We evaluate this group functional directly; mean-based response selection is
reported only as a secondary diagnostic
(Appendix~\ref{subsec:evaluation-endpoints}).

Fix a radius \(\varepsilon\in[0,1]\) before policy training and use the
one-sided envelope
\(\overline F_j(t)=\min\{1,F_{\omega,j}(t)+\varepsilon\}\), with
\(\overline p_{j,r}=\overline F_j(r)-\overline F_j(r-1)\).  Define
\(\mathfrak U_{\varepsilon}(p_{\omega,1:N})
:=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\{q_j\in\Delta(\mathcal R):
F_{q_j}(t)\le\overline F_j(t),\ t=0,\ldots,3\}\).  Set
\(\overline F_j(-1)=0,\overline F_j(4)=1\).  Increasing a CDF is adverse to an
upper-tail maximum, so the envelope changes the deployment target and its
policy credit together; it is not an auxiliary reward bonus. The radius
fixed in the recorded runs is \(\varepsilon_{\rm run}=0.0468083\).
Those results characterize this fixed ambiguity set; they do not validate a
population calibration guarantee. Appendix~\ref{app:theory} gives the corrected
calibration bound and distinguishes it from the recorded radius.

\subsection{Robust constrained objective}
The robust group value is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-group-value}
h_N^{\rm rob}
:=\inf_{q_{1:N}\in\mathfrak U_{\varepsilon}(p_{\omega,1:N})}
\mathbb E_q[\max_jR_j]
=\sum_{t=1}^{4}\Bigl[1-\prod_{j=1}^{N}\overline F_j(t-1)\Bigr].
\end{equation}
Our optimization objective is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-max-closed}
\max_{\theta}\ J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta)
:=\mathbb E[h_N^{\rm rob}(x,Y_{1:N})]
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
M(\theta)\ge m_0,\ Q(\theta)\ge q_0,\ K(\theta)\le\delta,
\end{equation}
where
\(M(\theta):=\mathbb E[\mu_\omega(x,Y)]\),
\(Q(\theta):=\mathbb E[Q_\eta(x,Y)]\), and
\[
K(\theta):=\frac{\mathbb E_{x,Y\sim\pi_\theta}[\sum_{h=1}^{L(Y)}k_\theta(s_h)]}
{\mathbb E_{x,Y\sim\pi_\theta}[L(Y)]},
\]
where \(L(Y)\ge1\) is response length and
\(k_\theta(s)=\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\Vert\pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot\mid s))\).
Thus \(K\) is a token-weighted population KL; rollout and held-out versions
use different state distributions (Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}).
The floors \(m_0,q_0\) are fixed from the reference policy and keep target-RM
and independent quality visible, while \(K\) measures policy motion separately.
In PPO, the floors enter through dual credits, the KL term through the
surrogate, and the distinct threshold \(\delta_{\rm hard}\) through batch
rollback; the measured token KL is an acceptance statistic, not a population
trust-region guarantee.  The Quality RM is independently trained but participates in policy optimization
through its floor. It is therefore a safeguard evaluator, not an untouched
external test; the generated-output proxy evaluators provide additional
diagnostics (Appendix~\ref{subsec:anti-exploitation}).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We first transform the evaluator distribution into a worst-case finite-budget
functional, then derive response-level credits and incorporate them into PPO.
The exact identities below characterize the unclipped target; the subsequent
controller specifies how tentative updates are accepted.

\subsection{Exact robust maximum and marginal credit}
\label{subsec:exact-credit}

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite-budget robust maximum]
\label{thm:main-max}
Under (A1), for every fixed \((x,Y_{1:N})\), an optimizer over the product
ambiguity set is \(q_j=\overline p_j\) for every \(j\), and the attained
worst-case Best-of-\(N\) value is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-max-theorem}
\inf_{q_{1:N}\in\mathfrak U_{\varepsilon}(p_{\omega,1:N})}
\mathbb E_q[\max_jR_j]
=\sum_{t=1}^{4}\left[1-\prod_{j=1}^{N}\overline F_j(t-1)\right].
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The key point is simultaneous optimality.  The maximum is coordinatewise
increasing, so saturating every admissible CDF upper bound makes all candidates
stochastically smallest at once; the adversary need not choose a different
perturbation for each threshold.  The full ambiguity-set problem therefore
reduces to four threshold products, without rating sampling or enumerating
\(5^N\) joint outcomes.

For a candidate \(i\), let \(\overline R_i\sim\overline p_i\) and
\(\overline M_{-i}=\max_{j\ne i}\overline R_j\), with empty maximum zero
when \(N=1\). Its leave-one-out marginal
credit is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-credit-closed}
q_i^{\rm rob}:=\mathbb E[(\overline R_i-
\overline M_{-i})_+\mid x,Y_{1:N}]
=\sum_{t=1}^{4}[1-\overline F_i(t-1)]\prod_{j\ne i}\overline F_j(t-1).
\end{equation}
The first factor is candidate \(i\)'s probability of crossing threshold \(t\);
the product over \(j\ne i\) is the probability that the other responses leave
that threshold uncovered.  This leave-one-out value captures incremental group
credit rather than a winner indicator, so a response can matter without having
the largest mean score.  Prefix and suffix products compute all credits in
\(O(N|\mathcal R|)\) time per prompt using the same kernel as the group
objective, without sampling latent ratings. It is a
marginal, rather than additive, decomposition: ties or redundant candidates
need not exhaust the group value, because the signal is intended to measure
incremental threshold coverage.

Changing \(N\) changes the group functional, not merely the number of reward
observations. The following identity applies likelihood-ratio differentiation
\citep{williams1992simple,sutton1999policy} to this fixed-budget target.

\begin{theorem}[Exact marginal policy credit]
\label{thm:main-gradient}
Under (A1)--(A3), the exact group objective has the score-function gradient
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-policy-gradient}
\nabla_\theta J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta)=\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_i^{\rm rob}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(Y_i\mid x)\right].
\end{equation}
Thus the response-level estimator is unbiased before PPO clipping.
\end{theorem}

The same cached probabilities define the robust target and every leave-one-out
credit. The likelihood ratio distributes each response's credit over its
tokens, without sampled ratings or a learned group-winner critic. PPO clipping,
normalization, KL regularization, and rollback modify the realized update;
the theorem does not assert that this practical update remains an unbiased
gradient estimator of \(J_N^{\rm rob}\).

\subsection{Constrained PPO update}
\label{subsec:constrained-credit}

For dual variables \(\lambda_m,\lambda_q\ge0\), the response-level credit used
by PPO is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:constrained-credit}
\widetilde q_i=q_i^{\rm rob}+\frac{\lambda_m}{N}\mu_\omega(x,Y_i)
 +\frac{\lambda_q}{N}Q_\eta(x,Y_i),\qquad
A_i=N[\widetilde q_i-b_\psi(x)].
\end{equation}
The factors \(1/N\) match the one-response constraints to the group objective;
the prompt-only baseline preserves the score-function identity.  We use the
standard clipped PPO surrogate \citep{schulman2017proximal} and
full-categorical token KL.  Writing
\(\rho_{i,h}=\pi_\theta(a_{i,h}\mid s_{i,h})/
\pi_{\rm old}(a_{i,h}\mid s_{i,h})\), the update maximizes the standard
per-token expression \(\min\{\rho_{i,h}A_i,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,h},1-\epsilon_{\rm PPO},1+\epsilon_{\rm PPO})A_i\}\)
summed over action tokens and averaged over candidates, minus
\(\beta_{\rm KL}\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}\), and divides
by the lagged RMS scale.  The full implementation, including projected dual
updates, is specified in Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}.

Before a tentative step, \method{} snapshots all policy parameters and the
complete optimizer state.  If the post-update KL on the same rollout states
exceeds \(\delta_{\rm hard}\), both snapshots are restored; baseline, dual,
RMS, and controller states advance only after acceptance.  Snapshotting the
optimizer moments is necessary for Adam-like optimizers, and the resulting
rollback count is comparable across baselines under the shared controller.
The soft target \(\delta\) and the hard threshold \(\delta_{\rm hard}\) serve
different purposes: the former is the reference-policy constraint represented
by the KL controller, whereas the latter is a batch-level reject rule.  An
accepted update can still differ from the population-constrained optimum, which
is why held-out KL is measured independently rather than inferred from the
rollback threshold.

\subsection{StableMax-PPO}
\label{subsec:algorithm}
Here \(\mathsf{Opt}\) denotes the complete policy-optimizer state, including
momentum and adaptive-variance buffers.  We compute
\(\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}\) as the batch mean of the full-categorical token KL on
rollout states, not from sampled actions.  The update uses \(q_i^{\rm rob}\) and
\(A_i\) from \cref{eq:robust-credit-closed,eq:constrained-credit}.  Each iteration
evaluates the frozen models on a fixed-size rollout and updates the policy and
controller. The models, CDF radius, floors, soft target \(\delta\), and hard
rollback threshold remain fixed by the protocol.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{StableMax-PPO update}
\label{alg:stablemax}
\KwIn{Prompts \(x\sim\mathcal D\); initial trainable policy \(\pi_\theta\);
frozen \(p_\omega,Q_\eta,\pi_{\rm ref}\); fixed \(N,\varepsilon,
m_0,q_0,\delta,\delta_{\rm hard}\).}
\KwOut{Final accepted policy parameters \(\theta\), optimizer state
\(\mathsf{Opt}\), and controller state
\((b_\psi,\lambda_m,\lambda_q,\beta_{\rm KL},s)\).}
Initialize \(\mathsf{Opt}\), \(b_\psi\), \(\lambda_m,\lambda_q\),
\(\beta_{\rm KL}\), and lagged RMS scale \(s\)\;
\For{$k=1,\ldots,T_{\rm pol}$}{
  Set \(\pi_{\rm old}\leftarrow\pi_\theta\) and roll out \(N\) responses per
  prompt; evaluate the frozen \(p_\omega,Q_\eta\)\;
  Compute \(q_i^{\rm rob}\) from \cref{eq:robust-credit-closed} and \(A_i\)
  from \cref{eq:constrained-credit}\;
  Snapshot \((\theta,\mathsf{Opt})\) and take one clipped-PPO step with
  coefficient \(\beta_{\rm KL}\)\;
  Measure \(\widehat{\rm KL}(\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\rm ref})\) on the same
  rollout states\;
  \eIf{$\widehat{\rm KL}>\delta_{\rm hard}$}{
    Restore \((\theta,\mathsf{Opt})\), discard the tentative update and its
    rollout, and do not advance \(b_\psi,\lambda_m,\lambda_q,\beta_{\rm KL},s\);
  }{
    Commit the transition; fit \(b_\psi\), project the dual updates, and update
    \(\beta_{\rm KL},s\) from accepted data\;
  }
}
\end{algorithm}
All quantities needed to reproduce the update are fixed above or in
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}; only the policy and controller states are
updated, and rejected transitions advance none of them.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

The previous section gives the exact target and its policy-gradient bridge.
Here we state the guarantees that explain the role of the calibration radius
and the update controller.  Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}.

\begin{theorem}[Calibration-robust lower bound]
\label{thm:coverage-summary}
Under (A1), let \(F_j^*\) be the true conditional CDF.  If
\(F_j^*(t)\le\overline F_j(t)\) for every candidate and
\(t\in\{0,1,2,3\}\), then
the true conditional maximum satisfies
\(\mathbb E_*[\max_jR_j\mid x,Y_{1:N}]\ge h_N^{\rm rob}(x,Y_{1:N})\).
Without exact coverage,
\(\mathbb E_*[\max_jR_j\mid x,Y_{1:N}]
\ge h_N^{\rm rob}(x,Y_{1:N})-4N\xi(x,Y_{1:N})\), where
\(\xi=\max_{j\in[N],\,t\in\{0,1,2,3\}}
[F_j^*(t)-\overline F_j(t)]_+\).
\end{theorem}

This is a conditional envelope statement, not a distribution-free guarantee:
even a valid population-average calibration bound does not establish
pointwise coverage for every generated response.  If the envelope misses by at most
\(\xi\), the four ordinal thresholds and \(N\) candidate CDFs contribute the
explicit \(4N\xi\) degradation term.  The factor depends on the declared
five-level support and product model, so it is a sensitivity certificate for
this evaluator interface rather than a claim of universal calibration.

The factor \(4N\) follows from the representation itself: at each of the four
nontrivial thresholds, changing one candidate CDF changes the corresponding
product by at most its CDF error.  Summing these \(N\)-candidate sensitivities
over thresholds gives the stated degradation, independently of the optimizer.

The radius also has a deterministic target-level interpretation independent of
how it is estimated.
\begin{proposition}[Calibration-radius monotonicity]
\label{prop:radius-monotonicity}
For \(0\le\varepsilon_1\le\varepsilon_2\le1\),
\(0\le J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta;\varepsilon_1)-J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta;\varepsilon_2)
\le4N(\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_1)\), and at \(\varepsilon=0\),
\(J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta;0)=J_N^{\rm nom}(\theta)\).
\end{proposition}
The radius is therefore an interpretable target-level knob: increasing it makes
the robust value more conservative with bounded sensitivity.  Unlike a KL
coefficient, it changes both the group target and its marginal credit, so the
nominal-EV comparator isolates a change in the evaluator-defined objective.  A
radius change is consequently not equivalent to retuning PPO: it changes the
policy-gradient direction as well as the objective level.

Let \(\phi_i=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(Y_i\mid x)\).  Consider a hypothetical
estimator that samples latent ratings from the robust envelope,
\(G_{\rm samp}=\sum_i(\overline R_i-\overline M_{-i})_+\phi_i\), and the
implemented credit estimator \(G_{\rm RB}=\sum_iq_i^{\rm rob}\phi_i\).  The
former introduces evaluator-sampling noise that can be removed by conditional
expectation \citep{casella1996rao}.

\begin{proposition}[Rao--Blackwell variance dominance]
\label{prop:variance}
Assume \(\mathbb E\|G_{\rm samp}\|^2<\infty\).
Conditioned on \((x,Y_{1:N})\), \(G_{\rm RB}=\mathbb E[G_{\rm samp}\mid
x,Y_{1:N}]\).  Consequently, for every vector \(v\),
\(\operatorname{Var}(v^\top G_{\rm RB})\le
\operatorname{Var}(v^\top G_{\rm samp})\).
\end{proposition}

The exact credit removes variance from drawing latent ordinal ratings while
leaving rollout and policy-sampling variance unchanged.  The PPO update thus
uses the conditional expectation of the group credit without extra evaluator
calls; the proposition makes no claim that total optimization variance vanishes.
It identifies one avoidable source of noise and leaves response-sampling,
optimization, and controller variability to the empirical analysis.

The last result concerns the realized optimizer state rather than the
population objective.
\begin{proposition}[Rollback correctness]
\label{prop:rollback}
If a tentative update is rejected, restoring the parameter and optimizer
snapshots exactly returns the measured rollout-state policy and optimizer to
their pre-update values.  This is a batch-level invariant, not a population KL
guarantee.
\end{proposition}
The envelope protects the model-conditional target, the marginal identity makes
it optimizable, and rollback controls the measured batch update.  Restoring the
complete snapshot is essential for optimizer and controller consistency, but
does not imply a population trust region after an accepted update; maximum,
quality, held-out KL, and rollback frequency are therefore reported separately.
In particular, restoring policy weights without adaptive buffers could make a
rejected transition affect the next step, so the invariant is stated for the
complete optimizer/controller state.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Our empirical question is whether the exact robust ordinal objective and its
policy credit yield a reproducible maximum--drift trade-off under matched
training and quality safeguards.
We hold the response budget, prompt stream, seeds, optimizer, and rollback rule
fixed, while comparators vary the deployment functional, calibration
treatment, or safeguard. We report robust maximum together with
independently modeled quality, held-out KL, and rollback frequency so that target
performance and policy stability remain separately observable.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
The policy is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct.  The frozen ordinal Moment RM is a
Qwen2.5-14B model trained on HelpSteer2 repeated helpfulness ratings, with the
decontaminated preference replay specified in Appendix~\ref{app:full-experiments}
\citep{wang2024helpsteer2}.  The independent scalar Quality RM is a separately
trained Qwen2.5-14B model fit on disjoint preference data.  We use
three confirmatory seeds \(\{314,2718,1618\}\), \(N=32\), and 512 held-out
prompts per seed.  Primary baselines are Mean-PPO + Q, GRPO + Q, and Scalar
Max@32-PPO + Q; full settings, ablations, and audit records are in
Appendix~\ref{app:full-experiments}.  We report robust maximum, held-out KL,
and rollback frequency because they measure the two sides of the proposed
operating point.

Repeated helpfulness ratings supervise the categorical output and its
disagreement diagnostic; the independent Quality RM and generated-output
proxies test transfer beyond the target evaluator.  Shared prompts, seeds,
generation budgets, and rollback thresholds keep every comparison on the same
response-sampling problem.  All confirmatory claims therefore concern this
fixed \(N=32\) operating point, not scale or budget generalization.

The primary comparators are chosen to isolate the two axes in the objective.
Mean-PPO keeps the conventional single-response target, GRPO keeps a
group-relative scalar target, and Scalar max-PPO uses the same finite budget
with the ordinal distribution collapsed to response means.  Matching their
optimizer, quality safeguards, and rollback rule makes the comparison about
the deployment functional and its credit assignment, rather than about a
different amount of computation or a different update stabilizer.  The
additional evaluator and floor variants in Appendix~\ref{app:full-experiments}
then vary one contract component at a time.

\subsection{Main results}
In Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}, \method{} reaches a robust expected maximum of $3.458$ and held-out KL of
$0.034$, compared with $3.424$ and $0.070$ for Mean-PPO. Under the shared
rollback controller it rejects 31.0\% of tentative updates, versus 58.6\% for
Mean-PPO and 82.3\% for GRPO.  GRPO and Scalar max-PPO attain higher robust
maxima, so the evidence supports a lower-drift operating point rather than
universal dominance.  The primary endpoint is the same $h_{32}^{\rm rob}$ group functional used
in training; it does not measure fresh human ratings of a selected response.

Relative to Mean-PPO, the robust-maximum point estimate is higher by 0.034,

Held-out KL measures full next-token divergence from the reference on common
base-policy trajectories (Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}). Lower KL thus means
less distributional departure on these evaluation states. This is the measured
drift-control benefit; quality is assessed separately by the declared floors
and independent proxies.

The frozen mean and quality floors pass their declared tests, but the complete
predeclared scientific-success criterion is not met
(Appendix~\ref{subsec:gate-outcomes}). Higher maxima for GRPO and scalar
max-PPO and the overlapping independent-proxy intervals delimit the claim:
these runs demonstrate an objective-aligned, lower-drift policy, not a
uniformly better policy or an established Pareto frontier.

\subsection{Ablation and stability analysis}
\label{subsec:ablation-stability-main}
Removing the mean floor raises robust maximum from 3.458 to 3.508 and
held-out KL from 0.034 to 0.048. Removing the quality floor gives
\((3.467,0.048)\), while removing CDF robustification gives
\((3.421,0.028)\), in (robust maximum, held-out KL) coordinates. Relative to either
no-floor variant, the full method has about 29\% lower aggregate KL and a lower
robust maximum. The robust variant has higher values on both axes than the
nominal ordinal variant, distinguishing the observed target trade-off from
that of the floors.
The complete aggregate and per-seed tables are in
Appendix~\ref{app:full-experiments}.
The three-seed intervals overlap and per-seed effects are heterogeneous, so
these are sensitivity analyses rather than isolated causal effects. Removing
CDF robustification changes the training objective; removing a floor changes
the accepted-update trajectory. No component is presented as a universally
improving reward term.

Together, the results answer the objective-design question at the evaluated
operating point: the robust ordinal credit trains against the same
finite-budget statistic used for evaluation, while quality floors and held-out
KL keep typical performance and policy movement visible.  Independent
generated-output diagnostics are reported in
Appendix~\ref{subsec:anti-exploitation}; they are model-based diagnostics
rather than fresh human judgments.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Scope.} The endpoint is induced by frozen evaluators, with no fresh human
labels for generated outputs; model proxies may disagree with people, the
1.5B/14B capacity gap may induce evaluator shift, and three seeds do not
establish scale or domain generalization.  We fix \(N=32\), and the exact
product form assumes conditionally independent rating outcomes, so correlated
annotation or evaluator errors are outside the guarantee.

\textbf{Future work.} Blinded human ratings, additional response budgets and
domains, larger policies, and correlated-error ambiguity sets are natural next
tests.  The last direction is especially important because it asks whether the
threshold construction can retain tractable policy credit when evaluator errors
are shared across candidates.
