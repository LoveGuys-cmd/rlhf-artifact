\title{StableMax-PPO: Finite-Budget RLHF with a Max--Stability Trade-off}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The deployment objective is a finite-budget order statistic, while the
evaluator exposes an uncertain ordinal distribution.  We study this mismatch
in terminal-reward RLHF with a frozen ordinal Moment RM trained from HelpSteer2
repeated helpfulness ratings and preference pairs.  Rather than collapsing this
bounded ordinal distribution to a scalar or Gaussian reward, we derive an exact
finite-support Best-of-\(N\) objective and a one-sided calibration correction
for its upper tail.  \method{} turns the resulting exact marginal credit into
a PPO update with mean and independent-quality floors, full-categorical KL
measurement, and batch-level rollback.  The design exposes a tunable operating
point: the method retains competitive finite-budget maximum performance while
reducing policy drift relative to mean- and group-relative baselines.  Its
contribution is therefore a principled max--stability trade-off, not a claim of
universal dominance or direct human-preference improvement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\paragraph{The deployment mismatch.}
Language models are increasingly deployed with a finite generation budget:
several responses are sampled and a downstream system returns, reranks, or
inspects the best candidate.  The deployment objective is therefore a
finite-budget order statistic, not the expected score of one response.  A
mean-improving update can leave the chance of a high-scoring candidate
unchanged, whereas direct maximum optimization can concentrate probability in a
small and brittle region of the response distribution.

\paragraph{Why current objectives leave a gap.}
Mean-PPO and GRPO provide scalar expected or group-relative feedback; recent
max@\(k\), Best-of-\(N\), and inference-aware policy-gradient methods match the
deployment budget but usually collapse the evaluator to a scalar or a nominal
distribution \citep{shao2024deepseekmath,guo2025deepseekr1,
chow2025inference,tang2025inference,walder2025pkpo,bagirov2025best,
li2026posttraining,poiani2026maxk}.  KL-regularized PPO controls policy motion
but does not specify which ordinal tail functional should be optimized.  These
choices leave a concrete gap: an upper-tail objective should preserve the
ordinal thresholds, account for calibration error where the maximum is most
sensitive, and expose ordinary quality and policy drift as separate measured
quantities.

\paragraph{StableMax-PPO.}
Our evaluator interface is a frozen Moment RM trained on the repeated
helpfulness ratings and preference pairs in HelpSteer2
\citep{wang2024helpsteer2}.  It returns a probability vector over
\(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\): the mean is an ordinary model-implied score, the conditional
variance is a diagnostic of disagreement under the repeated-annotation
protocol, and the full vector preserves every threshold used by a finite-budget
maximum.  We introduce \method{} by placing a one-sided calibration envelope
around this vector.  The envelope gives an exact robust maximum and, from the
same threshold products, an exact leave-one-out marginal credit.  A standard PPO
realization then adds an independent quality floor, a reference-policy KL
penalty, and exact batch rollback when a tentative update exceeds the hard KL
level.

The novelty is the resulting evaluator-to-optimizer contract, not the isolated
reuse of PPO or KL regularization.  The categorical evaluator determines the
threshold functional; the envelope specifies its adverse calibration
direction; and the same construction yields the response-level credit used by
the policy gradient.  The controller keeps this target, ordinary quality, and
policy motion separately auditable.  Consequently, a maximum gain obtained by
changing the evaluator regime, lowering typical quality, or moving far from the
reference appears on a different point of the measured frontier rather than
being hidden inside one scalar reward.  This coupling is the key distinction
from adding independent bonuses: the calibration choice changes both the
deployment functional and the credit that differentiates it.

We make four contributions: (1) an exact finite-\(N\) robust objective for a
bounded ordinal evaluator; (2) an exact Rao--Blackwellized marginal credit and
score-function identity; (3) a compact PPO controller with quality/KL
safeguards and state-complete rollback; and (4) an empirical characterization
of the max--stability frontier under shared evaluation and rollback controls.
The complete diagnostic record, including unfavorable outcomes, delimits this
frontier rather than defining a new success criterion.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:problem-setup}

We consider response-level RLHF with a fixed Best-of-\(N\) budget.  The policy
parameter \(\theta\) is trainable; the ordinal Moment RM \(\omega\), scalar
Quality RM \(\eta\), and reference policy \(\pi_{\rm ref}\) are frozen.  For
\(x\sim\mathcal D\), responses factor as
\(\pi_\theta(Y\mid x)=\prod_h\pi_\theta(a_h\mid x,a_{<h})\), and a rollout
contains \(Y_{1:N}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\).  Each response receives one
terminal evaluation.  We assume (A1) conditional independence of rating
outcomes across candidates, (A2) frozen evaluators, and (A3) the standard
differentiability and likelihood-ratio regularity.

\paragraph{Deployment target and learning signal.}
At deployment the group statistic is the maximum over the \(N\) candidates,
whereas policy optimization supplies response-level likelihood ratios.  The
learning signal must therefore preserve the interaction between candidates
before assigning credit to individual responses.  This is also why the
evaluators remain frozen: a change in the measured frontier should be
attributable to the policy and controller, not to a moving target.

\subsection{Frozen ordinal evaluator}
\label{subsec:ordinal-rm}

The Moment RM predicts \(p_{\omega,r}(x,y)=\Pr_\omega(R=r\mid x,y)\) for
\(r\in\mathcal R=\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), with \(\sum_r p_{\omega,r}=1\).  We retain
the categorical vector because the finite-budget maximum depends on every
threshold, use \(\mu_\omega=\sum_r r p_{\omega,r}\) for the ordinary-quality
floor, and record
\(\sigma_\omega^2=\sum_r(r-\mu_\omega)^2p_{\omega,r}\) as a model-based
disagreement diagnostic under the repeated-annotation protocol in HelpSteer2
\citep{wang2024helpsteer2}.  The independent scalar Quality RM supplies an
external quality check; the complete ordinal-probit parameterization is in
Appendix~\ref{app:theory}.

This evaluator choice is part of the method's interface: the five categories
are fixed by the annotation protocol, so the upper endpoint and threshold gaps
are not selected after observing policy outcomes.  The mean, variance, and
categorical vector consequently have separate roles rather than being collapsed
into one reward.  The variance diagnoses unstable evaluator regions but does not
enter the optimized target.

\subsection{Finite-budget objective}
\label{subsec:ordinal-objective}

For candidate \(j\), write \(F_{\omega,j}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{t}p_{\omega,r}(x,Y_j)\).
The nominal expected maximum has the exact finite-support form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ordinal-max-closed}
h_N^{\rm nom}(x,Y_{1:N})=
\sum_{t=1}^{4}\left[1-\prod_{j=1}^{N}F_{\omega,j}(t-1)\right].
\end{equation}
Thus rating noise is integrated out before policy optimization and no Gaussian
or sampled-max approximation is introduced.  The probability of at least one
rating-four response is a secondary diagnostic; the complete ordinal maximum
is the primary estimand (Appendix~\ref{app:full-experiments}).

The threshold form defines the signal that the policy must learn.  A candidate
matters when it reduces the probability that all other responses remain below a
threshold, so complementary upper-tail mass can receive credit even when no
unique sampled winner exists.  Two candidates with the same
\(\mu_\omega\) can therefore induce different group gains; a scalar max@32
objective cannot represent this distinction because it ranks only collapsed
scores.  The group statistic is converted to response-level credit only after
this complementarity has been computed.

\subsection{Calibration uncertainty}
\label{subsec:robust-objective}

Because the maximum magnifies upper-tail errors, we estimate a one-sided radius
from independent prompt clusters before training.  For threshold
\(t\in\{0,1,2,3\}\), let \(D_{g,t}\) be the within-cluster mean of
\(\ind{R\le t}-F_\omega(t)\), and let \(\widehat d_t\) be its mean across
clusters.  The frozen radius is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:epsilon-calibration}
\varepsilon_{\rm cal}=\min\!\left\{1,\max_{t=0,\ldots,3}(\widehat d_t)_+
 +\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\alpha_{\rm cal})}{2G_{\rm cal}}}\right\}.
\end{equation}
\begin{samepage}
For \(\mathcal U_\varepsilon(p)=\{q\in\Delta^4:F_q(t)\le
\min(1,F_p(t)+\varepsilon),\ t=0,\ldots,3\}\), let
\(\overline F_j(t)=\min(1,F_{\omega,j}(t)+\varepsilon_{\rm cal})\), with
\(\overline F_j(-1)=0\), \(\overline F_j(4)=1\), and induced mass
\(\overline p_{j,r}=\overline F_j(r)-\overline F_j(r-1)\).  The robust
objective is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-max-closed}
\begin{aligned}
h_N^{\rm rob}(x,Y_{1:N})
&=\inf_{q_j\in\mathcal U_{\varepsilon_{\rm cal}}(p_{\omega,j})}
\mathbb E_q[\max_jR_j]
 =\sum_{t=1}^{4}\left[1-\prod_{j=1}^{N}\overline F_j(t-1)\right],\\[-2pt]
J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta)&=\mathbb E_{x,Y_{1:N}}[h_N^{\rm rob}(x,Y_{1:N})].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{samepage}
The one-sided envelope is chosen for the maximum's adverse direction:
increasing a candidate CDF decreases the probability of crossing every
threshold.  It therefore adds low-rating mass without turning disagreement into
a reward.  Because the radius is fixed on a disjoint calibration split, it is a
measurable conservatism knob rather than a policy-dependent tuning preference;
when calibration error is small, the robust target approaches the nominal one.

\subsection{Constrained policy objective}
\label{subsec:constrained-objective}

The maximum can improve a few candidates while degrading typical responses or
over-optimizing the training evaluator.  We therefore optimize \(J_N^{\rm rob}\)
subject to \(M(\theta)=\mathbb E[\mu_\omega(x,Y)]\ge m_0\),
\(Q(\theta)=\mathbb E[Q_\eta(x,Y)]\ge q_0\), and
\(\mathbb E_x[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\rm ref})]\le\delta\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:constrained-problem}
\displaystyle \max_\theta J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
M(\theta)\ge m_0,\quad Q(\theta)\ge q_0,\quad
\mathbb E_x[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\rm ref})]\le\delta .
\end{equation}
The floors are fixed from the reference policy before training, while
\(\delta_{\rm hard}\) is the empirical rollback threshold used by the
controller.  The independent Quality RM and KL controller keep the measured
trade-off auditable under a fixed evaluator.

Each constraint addresses a different failure mode.  The nominal-mean floor
prevents a tail optimizer from sacrificing the typical response; the independent
Quality RM tests whether an improvement transfers beyond the training
evaluator; and the KL budget limits how much behavior can change while these
scores are being optimized.  We consequently report the maximum and drift as
separate axes instead of combining them into an arbitrary weighted sum.

The constraints are not folded into the robust maximum: the floors enter
through dual credits, the KL term enters the PPO surrogate, and rollback is a
binary acceptance rule.  We therefore report objective value, constraint
residuals, held-out drift, and rejected-step frequency as distinct diagnostics.

\paragraph{Relation to existing objectives.}
\method{} builds on PPO and prior inference-aware, pass@\(k\), max@\(k\), and
Best-of-\(N\) policy-gradient objectives
\citep{chow2025inference,tang2025inference,walder2025pkpo,
bagirov2025best,li2026posttraining,poiani2026maxk}; it does not claim the first
finite-budget maximum.  The comparator matrix instead isolates the proposed
contract: Mean-PPO and GRPO retain scalar expected or group-relative feedback,
Scalar Max@32 retains the budget but collapses the ordinal evaluator, nominal
EV removes only the calibration envelope, and no-floor variants remove one
safeguard.  Entropic and Gaussian variants test alternative distributional
assumptions.  This factorization distinguishes a change in the target from a
change in the controller, rather than presenting the method as an unexplained
stack of familiar PPO heuristics.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The method separates the deployment target from its stabilizers.  The target
is the robust ordinal maximum; the stabilizers control typical quality and
policy motion.  This separation makes the max--stability trade-off visible
instead of hiding it in a scalar reward weight.

\subsection{Exact robust maximum and marginal credit}
\label{subsec:exact-credit}

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite-budget robust maximum]
\label{thm:main-max}
Under (A1), the envelope distribution \(\overline p_j\) simultaneously attains
the infimum in \cref{eq:robust-max-closed} for every candidate.  Hence the
four-term expression is the exact worst-case expected maximum over the full
declared product ambiguity set, not a Monte Carlo or Gaussian approximation.
\end{theorem}

The theorem is useful computationally: it replaces an optimization over rating
tables by four threshold products, so the robust target is evaluated without
sampling latent ratings or enumerating \(5^N\) outcomes.  For a candidate \(i\),
let \(\overline R_i\sim\overline p_i\) and
\(\overline M_{-i}=\max_{j\ne i}\overline R_j\).  Its leave-one-out marginal
credit is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-credit-closed}
q_i^{\rm rob}:=\mathbb E[(\overline R_i-
\overline M_{-i})_+\mid x,Y_{1:N}]
=\sum_{t=1}^{4}[1-\overline F_i(t-1)]\prod_{j\ne i}\overline F_j(t-1).
\end{equation}
This is the paper's central credit construction: it integrates out rating noise
but preserves the response-level likelihood ratio, so the policy receives
credit only for thresholds at which a candidate can improve the group maximum.

This credit is deliberately leave-one-out rather than winner-take-all.  A
winner indicator would give zero signal to a response that raises the chance of
crossing a threshold without being the sampled winner, and would require an
additional rating draw for every rollout.  The product over \(j\ne i\) measures
how much room the other candidates leave at each threshold; the credit is thus
both group-aware and response-specific.  Prefix and suffix products evaluate
all \(N\) credits in \(O(N|\mathcal R|)\) time per prompt, which is the
computational reason the exact construction is usable inside PPO.

The scaling statement matters for the engineering use case.  A naive
implementation would reevaluate a group maximum after perturbing each
candidate, or enumerate rating tuples.  Threshold-wise prefix and suffix
products instead share intermediate values across all candidates.  Exactness
is therefore not only an analytical convenience: the deployment statistic and
its training credit are computed by the same small tensor kernel.

\begin{theorem}[Exact marginal policy credit]
\label{thm:main-gradient}
Under the regularity assumptions, \(q_i^{\rm rob}\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-policy-gradient}
\nabla_\theta J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta)=\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_i^{\rm rob}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(Y_i\mid x)\right].
\end{equation}
Consequently, the response-level estimator is unbiased before PPO clipping.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Why the identity matters.}
The score-function identity is the bridge from the group deployment target to a
trainable response-level update.  It integrates out latent rating draws, while
the autoregressive likelihood ratio distributes each candidate's group credit
over its tokens.  The same cached categorical probabilities therefore feed the
robust objective, all leave-one-out credits, and the disagreement diagnostic in
\(O(N|\mathcal R|)\) time.  Exactness is asserted for this pre-clipping target;
PPO then supplies the standard clipped surrogate and minibatch machinery.

\subsection{Constrained PPO update}
\label{subsec:constrained-credit}

For dual variables \(\lambda_m,\lambda_q\ge0\), the response-level credit used
by PPO is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:constrained-credit}
\widetilde q_i=q_i^{\rm rob}+\frac{\lambda_m}{N}\mu_\omega(x,Y_i)
 +\frac{\lambda_q}{N}Q_\eta(x,Y_i),\qquad
A_i=N[\widetilde q_i-b_\psi(x)].
\end{equation}
The factors \(1/N\) match the one-response constraints to the group objective;
the prompt-only baseline preserves the score-function identity.  We use the
standard clipped PPO surrogate and full-categorical token KL.  Writing
\(\rho_{i,h}=\pi_\theta(a_{i,h}\mid s_{i,h})/
\pi_{\rm old}(a_{i,h}\mid s_{i,h})\), the update maximizes the standard
per-token expression \(\min\{\rho_{i,h}A_i,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,h},1-\epsilon_{\rm PPO},1+\epsilon_{\rm PPO})A_i\}\)
averaged over candidates, minus \(\beta_k\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}\), and divides
by the lagged RMS scale.  The full implementation, including projected dual
updates, is specified in Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}.

Before a tentative step, \method{} snapshots all policy parameters and the
complete optimizer state.  If the post-update KL on the same rollout states
exceeds \(\delta_{\rm hard}\), both snapshots are restored; baseline, dual,
RMS, and controller states advance only after acceptance.  Snapshotting the
optimizer moments is necessary for Adam-like optimizers, and the resulting
rollback count is comparable across baselines under the shared controller.

\subsection{StableMax-PPO}
\label{subsec:algorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{StableMax-PPO update}
\label{alg:stablemax}
\KwIn{$\mathcal D,\pi_\theta,\pi_{\rm ref},p_\omega,Q_\eta,\varepsilon_{\rm cal},m_0,q_0,N,\delta_{\rm hard}$}
\KwOut{Updated policy parameters $\theta$.}
Initialize $b_\psi,\lambda_m,\lambda_q,\beta_{\rm KL}$ and lagged scale $s>0$\;
\For{$k=1,\ldots,T_{\rm pol}$}{
  Roll out $N$ responses per prompt under $\pi_{\rm old}$\;
  Compute $\widetilde q_i$ and $A_i$ from the closed-form credits\;
  Snapshot $(\theta,\mathsf{Opt})$ and perform one clipped-PPO update\;
  \eIf{measured post-update $\widehat{\rm KL}>\delta_{\rm hard}$}{
    Restore $(\theta,\mathsf{Opt})$; leave controller state unchanged\;
  }{
    Commit and update $b_\psi,\lambda_m,\lambda_q,\beta_{\rm KL},s$\;
  }
}
\end{algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:stablemax} is the only algorithm in the paper; the
implementation constants and diagnostics are in the supplementary protocol.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

The previous section gives the exact target and its policy-gradient bridge.
Here we state the guarantees that explain the role of the calibration radius
and the update controller.  Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}.

\subsection{Calibration coverage}
\label{subsec:theory-calibration}

\begin{samepage}
\begin{theorem}[Calibration-robust lower bound]
\label{thm:coverage-summary}
Let \(F_j^*\) be the true conditional CDF.  If
\(F_j^*(t)\le\overline F_j(t)\) for every candidate and
\(t\in\{0,1,2,3\}\), then
the true conditional maximum satisfies
\(\mathbb E_*[\max_jR_j\mid x,Y_{1:N}]\ge h_N^{\rm rob}(x,Y_{1:N})\).
Without exact coverage, the gap is at most \(4N\xi(x,Y_{1:N})\), where
\(\xi=\max_{j,t}[F_j^*(t)-\overline F_j(t)]_+\).
\end{theorem}
\end{samepage}

The statement is conditional on pointwise envelope coverage.  Cluster-level
calibration estimates the radius but does not establish coverage for every
generated response; the residual \(\xi\) quantifies the resulting loss when the
envelope misses.  Thus the theorem supplies an operational lower-tail bound
under an explicit assumption rather than an informal reliability claim.

\subsection{Dependence on the calibration radius}
\label{subsec:radius-analysis}
\begin{proposition}[Calibration-radius monotonicity]
\label{prop:radius-monotonicity}
For \(0\le\varepsilon_1\le\varepsilon_2\le1\),
\(0\le J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta;\varepsilon_1)-J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta;\varepsilon_2)
\le4N(\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_1)\), and at \(\varepsilon=0\),
\(J_N^{\rm rob}(\theta;0)=J_N^{\rm nom}(\theta)\).
\end{proposition}
The proposition makes \(\varepsilon_{\rm cal}\) an interpretable target-level
knob: increasing it can only make the robust maximum more conservative, with a
finite sensitivity bound.  This is distinct from tuning a KL coefficient,
which changes the optimization path while leaving the target functional fixed.

\subsection{Rollback invariant}
\label{subsec:rollback-analysis}
\begin{proposition}[Rollback correctness]
\label{prop:rollback}
If a tentative update is rejected, restoring the parameter and optimizer
snapshots exactly returns the measured rollout-state policy and optimizer to
their pre-update values.  This is a batch-level invariant, not a population KL
guarantee.
\end{proposition}
These results separate the method's roles: the envelope protects the target,
the marginal identity makes it optimizable, and rollback controls the measured
batch update.  The first two are model-conditional statements about the
declared evaluator and independence model; the last is an implementation
invariant.  We therefore treat calibration residuals, held-out KL, and
rollback frequency as separate engineering diagnostics.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
The policy is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct.  The frozen ordinal Moment RM and
independent Quality RM are Qwen2.5-14B models trained with HelpSteer2 repeated
helpfulness ratings and preference pairs \citep{wang2024helpsteer2}.  We use
three confirmatory seeds \(\{314,2718,1618\}\), \(N=32\), and 512 held-out
prompts per seed.  Primary baselines are Mean-PPO + Q, GRPO + Q, and Scalar
Max@32-PPO + Q; full settings, ablations, and audit records are in
Appendix~\ref{app:full-experiments}.  We report robust maximum, held-out KL,
and rollback frequency because they measure the two sides of the proposed
operating point.

\subsection{Main results}
\method{} reaches a robust expected maximum of $3.458$ and held-out KL of
$0.034$, compared with $3.424$ and $0.070$ for Mean-PPO.  Under the shared
rollback controller it rejects 31.0\% of tentative updates, versus 58.6\% for
Mean-PPO and 82.3\% for GRPO.  GRPO and Scalar max-PPO attain higher robust
maxima, so the evidence supports a lower-drift operating point rather than
universal dominance.

0.020 less KL.  These are the two axes that the method exposes; collapsing them
into one weighted score would conceal the engineering trade-off the experiment
is designed to measure.

\subsection{Ablation and stability analysis}
Removing the mean floor increases robust max to $3.508$ but raises held-out KL
to $0.048$; removing the independent quality floor yields $3.467$ at KL
$0.048$.  Removing calibration robustification yields $3.421$ at KL $0.028$.
These ablations move along the same frontier and support interpreting the
constraints as operating-point controls, not uniformly beneficial reward terms.
Per-seed values and the remaining diagnostics are in the appendix.

Table~\ref{tab:component-ablation} makes the component interpretation
explicit.  Removing the mean floor buys maximum performance at a measurable
drift cost, while removing calibration changes the target itself and therefore
cannot be interpreted as a pure optimizer ablation.  The independent Quality
floor has a smaller endpoint effect but still changes the accepted-update
trajectory.  These comparisons are why we present \method{} as a controlled
operating point: each safeguard has a distinct failure mode and none should be
described as a universally improving reward term.

\subsection{Independent evaluator diagnostics}
\label{subsec:independent-evaluators}
To test whether improvements under the target Moment RM transfer to an
independent evaluation regime, we compare prompt-matched outputs from
\method{} and Mean-PPO with two frozen evaluators.  For each of the three
seeds, we form up to 512 pairs of held-out groups, select one response per
policy by $\arg\max_i\mu_\omega(x,Y_i)$, and score the selected responses with a
Qwen2.5-14B judge and \texttt{RLHFlow/ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1}.  The judge
order is randomized and checked in reverse; each evaluator is calibrated on a
frozen RewardBench split before it is used.  The reported preference is
tie-adjusted ($0.5$ is parity), with the interval obtained by the same
three-seed hierarchical bootstrap used for the primary endpoint.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The endpoint is induced by frozen evaluators and no fresh human labels are
collected for generated outputs.  Model proxies may disagree with humans, the
1.5B/14B capacity gap may induce evaluator shift, and three seeds do not
establish scale or domain generalization.  The exact product form also assumes
conditionally independent rating outcomes across sampled responses and does
not model correlated annotation or evaluator errors.  The artifact reports
these limitations and all unfavorable gates rather than redefining the
protocol.

\paragraph{Reproducibility statement.} The supplementary artifact contains the
source, official ICLR style files, protocol, result tables, verification JSON,
build commands, and the anonymous repository at
\url{https://github.com/LoveGuys-cmd/rlhf-artifact}.  The main paper specifies
the objective, algorithm, seeds, metrics, and aggregate results; appendices
give proofs and the complete protocol.  Model weights, credentials, private
data, and other non-redistributable files are excluded.

\paragraph{AI use statement.} Generative AI tools assisted with paper
organization, methodological exposition, language editing, LaTeX and figure
formatting, and debugging of analysis code.  The authors independently checked
all mathematical claims and proofs, verified implementation and numerical
values against frozen result files, and reviewed every AI-assisted change.  No
AI system generated experimental data or replaced author verification.

\paragraph{Ethics statement.} The study uses model-generated text and existing
preference data.  It collects no new human-subject data or personal
information.  Model-based preference proxies are reported as proxies, not as
human judgments of newly generated outputs; possible reward-model exploitation
and evaluator shift are disclosed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} and
Appendix~\ref{app:full-experiments}.
